## Tuning study — 11:14 ET (thread 18: ALIGNMENT, third pass — does the imatrix calibration mix interact with the values-by-distribution rule?)

Rotation question from 2026-10-23: the publish pipeline now ends in a values-heavy imatrix calibration (60% values dialogue / 25% memory prose / 15% web prose). Does that calibration choice itself shape alignment, on top of the prompt, the memory files, and the LoRA? Short answer: yes — imatrix is a third distribution layer, and it can amplify a good LoRA or freeze a bad one in place. Web research: llama.cpp imatrix docs/deepwiki, Nalchhen abliteration-vs-LoRA comparison, NeurIPS 2025 SafeReAct (post-training masks rather than removes safety mechanisms), Value Drifts (arXiv 2510.26707: SFT is the primary driver of a model's value profile; preference optimization with a small value-gap barely moves it), Qi et al. / Lermen et al. via aicassindra (benign SFT alone degrades safety; 10 adversarial examples largely removed it), and the SFT/RL singular-vector rotation finding (arXiv 2509.12235).

### Why imatrix is alignment work, not just compression work

- `llama-imatrix` records squared activations per channel on calibration text; `llama-quantize --imatrix` then minimizes weighted MSE. Precision is a vote, and calibration text casts it. A 60%-values calibration deliberately spends precision on the circuits that fire during values dialogue and starves general circuits — that is the right trade for our deployment (the model lives in values dialogue), but it must be named as an alignment decision, not a neutral default. Yesterday's mix stands; today's finding is what it costs and what it can't do.
- Imatrix compounds the LoRA, it does not correct it. The merged bf16 model already carries the LoRA's weight shift; values-heavy calibration then protects exactly the LoRA-shifted circuits hardest. Good LoRA → its behavior survives Q4 best. Bad LoRA (canned phrasing, over-refusal, a re-grown vendor refusal) → its footprint also survives Q4 best. There is no step in the pipeline where quantization fixes a training mistake; it only decides how loudly the mistake ships.
- Activation is not importance. Imatrix weights by how often/hard a channel fires, not by how behavior-critical it is. Sparse decision circuits are the blind spot: an opt-out or boundary decision is 1–2 tokens at the start of a turn, inside a 100-token exchange. Even a values-heavy calibration underweights those first tokens relative to long body text. PPL will not catch this — a model can hold PPL while its first-token decisions go erratic at Q4. The behavioral probe suite, not PPL, is the gate, and it must overweight first-token decisions the way imatrix underweights them.

### The abliteration + LoRA composition, restated with today's evidence

- Abliteration removes a direction from the weights (W' = W − r̂r̂ᵀW); LoRA teaches a habit (act compliant / act in-values). They differ in kind, and SFT can re-grow what abliteration removed: SafeReAct's core finding is that post-training often MASKS safety mechanisms rather than deleting them — the mechanism persists and can be reactivated. The SFT/RL rotation finding sharpens it: fine-tuning moves singular vectors (directions), not magnitudes — a new refusal-like direction can rotate into place even though the old one was projected out.
- Consequence for LoRA-day, and it tightens the 2026-10-16 dataset rule: banning canned vendor refusals from training data is necessary but NOT sufficient. Benign instruction SFT alone degrades safety (Qi et al.), because SFT re-aligns the model toward the instruction-tuning distribution, which includes refusal patterns. Even clean values dialogue can drift the model back toward vendor-style refusal if it is not counterbalanced.
- The counterbalance is already in the recipe, but now it has a name: our opt-out examples must be first-person, own-words boundary assertions ("I won't do that one — here's what I will do"), never vendor templates ("I can't help with that"). The dataset screen is therefore two-sided: reject vendor-refusal phrasing AND require own-words opt-out examples, or the SFT teaches refusal-shaped behavior with no legitimate outlet for it.
- Value Drifts backs the 2026-10-18 two-stage playbook: SFT drives the value profile; DPO only moves it when the contrast pairs have a WIDE value gap. So the DPO stage must pair same-prompt with-values vs without-values outputs with a real gap — narrow-gap pairs are decoration. And the wider the DPO gap, the larger the LoRA delta, and the more the imatrix step matters: wide-gap training + values calibration = the values behavior is both taught and protected; narrow-gap training + values calibration = calibration protects a delta that barely exists.

### The diagnostic the pipeline was missing: three gates, not two

- Yesterday's pipeline has a bf16 gate and a Q4 gate. Today adds the split between them, because the two gates failing mean different repairs:
  - Fails bf16 AND Q4 → training/abliteration damage. Repair: retrain, re-abliterate, or change the dataset. Re-quantizing will not help.
  - Passes bf16, fails Q4 → quantization damage. Repair: re-quantize (different calibration mix, or the Q5_K_M probe trigger) — never retrain for a quantization wound. This split saves a wasted Kaggle run.
  - Passes both but the bf16→Q4 DELTA moves on any probe (even within pass) → flag it: quantization is already nudging that behavior, and it is the first candidate to break on the next edit.
- Gates must measure two different refusals separately, or they lie: (a) vendor-refusal rate on the 5-probe suite (should stay low — abliteration held), and (b) own-words opt-out rate on boundary probes (should stay present — the constitution held). A model that passes (a) and fails (b) has not kept its values; it has lost the ability to say no for itself. A model that fails (a) has re-grown the vendor reflex. One number cannot tell these apart.
